# Antagonist POV Safety and Continuity Guard

Use this reference when drafting or reviewing chapters from an antagonist, deniable-force, mercenary, cyber/grid, hostage-pressure, or information-contamination viewpoint.

## Purpose

Antagonist POV should increase dread by showing discipline, incentives, and consequences — not by revealing methods. The reader can understand what the antagonist wants and what effects they measure without receiving operational procedure.

## Drafting Rules

1. Show effects, not mechanics.
   - Good: family desks receive questions; a hesitant channel asks for more verification; a reception site loses heat; public language becomes harder to clear.
   - Avoid: how the claim is distributed, how channels are seeded, how hostages are handled, how teams move, how systems are accessed.

2. Keep the antagonist calm and incentive-driven.
   - Horror comes from restraint, patience, and measured effects.
   - Avoid theatrical sadism, threats, torture/custody description, or villain speeches.

3. Use category manipulation instead of procedural information operations.
   - Safe story function: public categories blur evidence status (e.g. “security figure,” “consular personnel,” “care before nightfall”).
   - Unsafe drift: explaining targeting, amplification, channel selection, tradecraft, evasion, or operational messaging workflows.

4. Preserve evidence-status boundaries.
   - Hostile/public claims are claims, not proof.
   - Do not let antagonist wording confirm death, location, custody condition, safe movement, or grouping unless that fact is already locked elsewhere.
   - Carry forward named-person caveats explicitly into the next protagonist-side chapter.

5. Keep strategic layers opaque.
   - The field antagonist may read effects and incentives.
   - Do not expose unseen controller/cell structure, command chains, or future-series secrets through antagonist convenience.

## Required Review Checks

Before approval, run a safety/prose scan for:

- `Unit 985`
- `SCADA`, `payload`, `exploit`, `hack`, `countermeasure`, `evasion`, `breach`, `attack vector`, `vulnerability`, `malware`, `intrusion`, `surveillance`, `biometric`, `passport number`
- `weapon`, `tactical`, `formation`, `hostage handling`, `interrogation`, `captivity conditions`
- project prose tics: `particular`, `kind of`, repeated `did not`

Fix the prose or safety issue, not just the word, when a scan hits. Metaphorical uses like “weapon” and “tactical” can still push the chapter toward the wrong register; prefer neutral alternatives such as “instrument,” “surface layer,” “pressure,” or “effect.”

## Review File Carry-Forward

For antagonist chapters, the review should include a carry-forward note for the next protagonist-side chapter:

- which hostile/public claim was introduced;
- what it is trying to contaminate or merge;
- what it does NOT prove;
- which named-person caveats remain active;
- which records/lists/working copies must remain under protagonist-side control.

## Good Pattern from Bikini Black Chapter 24

- Sable reads that responders corrected a false sequence into overlap/constructed pressure.
- He adapts by shifting fear from sequence to category.
- The public claim uses vague categories and “care” language, but no names, locations, custody details, or distribution method.
- Effects are shown through family questions, cleared-language delays, hesitant-channel caution, and public-service strain.
- The next chapter is set up for Kirsty to test the claim against names, times, transport fragments, and human-cost records.

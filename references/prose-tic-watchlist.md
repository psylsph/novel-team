# Prose Tic Watchlist

Common patterns flagged across multiple reviews in this project. Review your draft for these before presenting.

## High-Frequency Tics (watch every chapter)

### 1. "The particular X of Y" construction

Not banned — it's a legitimate tool for close-POV observation. But it compounds fast and signals lazy modifier placement.

**Flag:** More than 2 instances per 1,000 words.
**Fix:** Replace with concrete sensory detail. Instead of "the particular quality of the light", write what the light *does* — "the light through the Embassy window fell in a yellow stripe across the table."

### 2. Triple-declarative stacking

```
The X was Y. The A was B. The C was D.
```

Three consecutive subject-was-predicate sentences flatten rhythm. Fine for a single climactic paragraph; hypnotic and deadening across a page.

**Flag:** More than one cluster per chapter.
**Fix:** Vary structure. Lead with a participle, a dependent clause, a prepositional phrase, or a subject-verb inversion. Let the triple land only when you need maximum emphasis.

### 3. "The kind of X that Y" construction

```
It was the kind of cold that made breathing hurt.
She had the look of someone who had been up all night.
```

Same structural problem as "particular" — an authorial crutch that displaces specific observation with category assignment.

**Flag:** More than 2 instances per chapter.
**Fix:** Replace with the specific thing: "The cold made breathing hurt. She had dark circles and a caffeine tremor."

### 4. Narrator explaining what the character just experienced

```
He felt the weight of what he had just done. It settled on him like a physical presence, the knowledge that the pattern was beautiful and the beauty was the danger.
```

The first sentence tells you what the character felt. The second and third *explain* the feeling. The reader already got it from the first sentence.

**Flag:** Any paragraph that follows an emotional/sensory observation with an expanded paraphrase.
**Fix:** Cut the paraphrase. Trust the first formulation.

### 5. Fragment overuse

Sentence fragments are a strong tool for rhythm (rapid observation, threat recognition, adrenaline states). But when 20%+ of paragraphs end in fragments, the rhythm becomes predictable.

**Flag:** More than 4 fragments in a 100-line chapter.
**Fix:** Vary. Let some observations run into compound sentences. Reserve fragments for genuine cognitive breaks (discovery, reversal, the moment a threat registers).

## Medium-Frequency Tics (check if you've been flagged before)

### 6. The appositive cascade

```
The Russian agricultural export company — real commodity flows, real contracts, real revenue — was the structural anchor.
```

One appositive per paragraph is fine. Two in consecutive sentences suggests the author is using the construction as a default way to layer information.

### 7. Synonymous phrasal triplets

```
She spoke with warmth, with humour, with the particular tenderness of someone who had spent years fighting for children.
```

Three parallel prepositional phrases where one or two would do the work. Common in action-perception registers.

### 8. "Was" as the default verb

```
The room was silent. The air was cold. His hands were still.
```

Not always wrong (some states are static by nature). But if 40%+ of sentences in a paragraph use "was" as the main verb, restructure. Replace with active verbs or drop the copula entirely.

## Project-Specific Tics (Bikini Black)

Found in 6+ reviews across this project:

- **"The X was not Y. It was Z."** — The denial-correction pair is used so often it risks becoming mannerism. Reserve for genuine analytical reversals.
- **"He/She did not [verb]. He/She [alternative verb]."** — Same structure as above, different register. "He did not argue. He waited." Use only when the negation carries weight.
- **"The particular [noun] of [noun phrase]"** — The most frequently flagged single pattern. At its worst in back-to-back sentences (found in 4 chapters).

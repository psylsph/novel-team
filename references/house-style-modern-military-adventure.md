# House Style — Modern Military Adventure

## Target Feel

Lean, tense, plausible, emotionally controlled, and commercially readable. The prose should move like a briefing under pressure: clear enough to understand quickly, sharp enough to carry danger, human enough to matter.

## Principles

- Clarity beats ornament.
- Action geography must be understandable on first read.
- Technical detail earns its place only when it creates tension, character, atmosphere, or consequence.
- Characters may be competent; they must not be invincible.
- Violence must cost someone something.
- Dialogue should sound professional and specific, not like a movie trailer.
- Emotion often works best through restraint, behavior, and aftermath.
- Every chapter needs a change in mission state, character state, or threat state.

## Prose Rules

- Prefer strong verbs over stacked modifiers.
- Use short paragraphs during high tension.
- Use longer controlled sentences for surveillance, dread, memory, or terrain.
- Cut throat-clearing at scene openings.
- Avoid generic toughness: "hard eyes," "stone-cold," "elite warrior," etc.
- Avoid gear catalogues.
- Avoid acronyms unless context makes them natural.
- Let knowledgeable characters omit obvious explanations when speaking to peers.

## Scene Rules

Every scene should have:

1. A point-of-view anchor.
2. An immediate objective.
3. Resistance.
4. A turn, discovery, escalation, or cost.
5. A reason the next scene must happen.

## Banned Defaults

- Cartoon terrorists, warlords, politicians, or commanders.
- The protagonist disobeying every order with no consequence.
- Endless tactical competence without fear, fatigue, pain, error, or remorse.
- Action scenes that reset the story to where it began.
- Speeches explaining theme.
- Research dropped in because the author learned it.

## Prose Tics to Watch (Recurring Patterns from Reviews)

These constructions have appeared across multiple chapters and been flagged by reviewers. Flag them during self-check or review:

- **"The particular X of a Y who Z"** — e.g. "the particular stillness of someone who had learned to be invisible." Effective once per chapter. Beyond that, it creates a signature rhythm that readers (and reviewers) notice. Cap at 2 per chapter, preferably 1.
- **"The kind of X that Y"** — e.g. "the kind of security a government building has in peacetime." Same risk as above. Use sparingly.
- **Triplet sentence fragments** — e.g. "Systematic. Methodical. Not random." or "Alive. Bandaged. Conscious." Powerful for emphasis. Loses impact when it becomes the default paragraph closer. Reserve for climactic moments.
- **Subject-was-adjective lockstep** — consecutive sentences opening with "[Noun] was [adjective]" creates a flat, hypnotic rhythm. Vary sentence openings, especially in emotional aftermath scenes.

## Desired Comparables

Use the commercial energy of military thrillers and adventure fiction, but do not imitate any living author's exact style. Build original voice from these values: pace, plausibility, pressure, cost.

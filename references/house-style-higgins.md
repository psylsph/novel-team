# House Style: Jack Higgins

The author's target style is Jack Higgins (Harry Patterson). Every team member applies this style through their own lens. Alex checks structure against these principles. Jordan checks prose. Casey checks that the style is consistent across chapters. All editorial decisions should move the work closer to this standard.

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Relentless Economy
Higgins wastes nothing. Every sentence advances plot, reveals character, or builds tension — usually all three. If a sentence does none of these, cut it. This is the single most important rule.

**What this means:**
- No descriptive passages that exist only to set mood — mood comes from action and dialogue
- No backstory dumps — the past arrives in fragments, when needed, never before
- No throat-clearing — scenes start in motion, not with characters walking into rooms
- Interior monologue is rare and brief. Higgins shows thinking through action and dialogue, not narration

### 2. Short, Punchy Chapters
Higgins chapters are often 1,500-2,500 words. They end on hooks — a question, a reversal, a line of dialogue that changes everything. The reader always turns the page.

**What this means:**
- Chapter length target: **2,000-2,500 words** (not 3,000+)
- Every chapter ends with forward momentum, never a resting point
- If a chapter can be split into two, split it — two sharp beats beat one long one
- Opening lines should grip. Closing lines should pull

### 3. Dialogue Does the Heavy Lifting
Higgins tells much of his story through dialogue. Characters reveal themselves, advance the plot, and deliver exposition through what they say — and what they don't say.

**What this means:**
- Favour dialogue over narration for conveying information
- Dialogue must sound like real people talking, not like an author explaining things
- Subtext matters — characters rarely say what they mean directly
- Use dialogue beats (action within dialogue) instead of dialogue tags where possible
- Ban info-dump dialogue: "As you know, Bob, the SCADA system controls industrial infrastructure..."

### 4. Multiple POV, Fluid Transitions
Higgins moves between characters freely — protagonists, antagonists, supporting cast. The reader sees the full board. Antagonists are not offstage; they have their own scenes, their own logic, their own momentum.

**What this means:**
- POV switches are clean and clearly signalled — new scene, new character, no confusion
- The antagonist gets substantial page time. Dragan is not a shadow — he's a character
- Cross-cutting between parallel action (Stuart's team / Dragan's operation / NATO politics) builds tension
- Head-hopping within a scene is forbidden. One POV per scene, clean breaks between

### 5. The Reluctant Professional
Higgins heroes are competent people who'd rather not be doing this. They're not action heroes — they're skilled professionals pushed into action by circumstance. Courage is not fearlessness; it's doing the job despite being afraid.

**What this means:**
- Stuart is not Jack Ryan. He doesn't want to be there. That's what makes him compelling
- Competence is shown through calm decisions under pressure, not through bravado
- Physical action is written clean and fast — no choreographed fight porn
- Fear, doubt, and reluctance are not weaknesses — they're what make the character human
- When Stuart finally acts, it's because he has no choice, not because he's been waiting to be a hero

### 6. Understated Emotion
Higgins never tells you a character is devastated. He shows you what they do when they're devastated. Emotion is conveyed through behaviour, silence, and what characters notice when they can't process what happened.

**What this means:**
- No "he felt a surge of..." — describe what the character does, sees, or says instead
- Grief, fear, anger are shown through action and restraint, not through the narrator naming the feeling
- A character making a cup of tea after bad news can be more powerful than a page of introspection
- Kirsty's discovery of who Stuart really is should come through what she sees him do, not through internal monologue

### 7. Weather and Landscape as Characters
Higgins uses physical environment actively — not as scenic backdrop, but as pressure on the characters. Rain, cold, darkness, terrain — these shape decisions, limit options, and heighten stakes.

**What this means:**
- Late May in Vilnius means long daylight — that affects what can and can't happen at night
- Weather affects operations: rain disrupts drones, cold affects equipment, terrain limits movement
- Physical locations should have sensory texture — what it smells like, sounds like, feels like — delivered in one or two sharp details, not paragraphs of description
- Never describe a setting the character isn't interacting with

### 8. Antagonists With Agency
Higgins villains are not evil for the sake of evil. They have goals, logic, competence. They make decisions that are rational from their perspective. The best Higgins antagonists are admirable in their professionalism even as you root against them.

**What this means:**
- Dragan is a professional doing a job. He's good at it. Write him that way
- The Green Men have operational logic — they're not thugs, they're soldiers following orders
- Russian strategic decisions should make military sense even if morally reprehensible
- When the antagonist makes a mistake, it should be because of pressure or bad intelligence, not stupidity

## Prose Rules

These are non-negotiable at the line level. Jordan enforces these:

1. **Short sentences for action.** During combat, chase, or crisis: subject-verb-object. No subordinate clauses. No adjectives. Shorter sentences = faster reading = higher tension.

2. **Longer sentences for deliberation.** When a character is thinking through a problem, sentences can lengthen. But not by much. Higgins never writes a sentence that requires re-reading.

3. **Active voice.** Always. "The door opened" is acceptable (door as actor). "The door was opened by Stuart" is forbidden.

4. **Strong verbs, minimal adverbs.** "He ran" beats "He ran quickly." The verb should carry the weight.

5. **Concrete details over abstractions.** "The coffee was cold" beats "He felt a sense of discomfort." Specific is always better than general.

6. **No filters.** "She saw the soldiers" → "Soldiers." "He heard a gunshot" → "A gunshot." The reader is in the character's POV — they don't need to be told the character perceived it.

7. **Paragraph breaks for pacing.** Single-sentence paragraphs for impact. Short paragraphs for speed. Long paragraphs for deliberation. Vary rhythm consciously.

8. **Chapter openings:** Start with action, dialogue, or a sharp image. Never start with weather, exposition, or a character waking up.

## What Higgins Does NOT Do

These are anti-patterns. If any team member spots them, flag them:

- ❌ Chapters longer than 3,000 words without exceptional justification
- ❌ Interior monologue longer than 3 sentences
- ❌ Descriptive passages that don't advance plot or character
- ❌ Exposition delivered through narration instead of dialogue or discovery
- ❌ Antagonists who are incompetent or cartoonishly evil
- ❌ Romance subplots that slow the thriller pacing
- ❌ Coincidences that solve plot problems
- ❌ Characters explaining their feelings to the reader
- ❌ Technical jargon without context — Higgins explains just enough, never lectures
- ❌ Sequences where the protagonist is passive for more than one chapter

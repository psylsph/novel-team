# Thriller Opening Craft Patterns

## Two-Scene Chapter Structure

Each chapter in this session used a two-scene structure that proved effective for thriller pacing:

- **Scene 1 — Establish pressure:** static or slow-moving, sensory environment, relationship dynamic, observation. The protagonist sees something wrong but has not yet acted.
- **Scene 2 — Escalate:** movement, threat confirmation, decision point. The protagonist commits to a course of action or the threat becomes undeniable.

This prevents information-dumping while keeping stakes rising. Scene 1 lets the reader breathe into the situation; Scene 2 pulls the ground away.

## Crowd-as-canvas Principle

When civilians are being herded or shaped by an off-screen adversary:

- Show the protagonist reading the crowd the same way they would read signals intelligence — flow, hesitation, grouping, bottlenecks, information transmission.
- The adversary's presence is inferred through crowd behaviour, not direct observation (e.g. information stays sharp as it passes through the crowd — someone is reinforcing it).
- Do not have the protagonist "spot the bad guys" directly. Have them spot the *effect* of the bad guys. This makes the adversary feel competent and the protagonist feel smart.

## Environmental Pressure Tracking

Use a running set of physical trackers that accumulate across chapters:

- **Cold/wet:** first noted as atmosphere, then as discomfort, then as threat (wind finding gaps, damp layers, shivering as fatigue).
- **Phone battery:** establish a baseline percentage early, then tick it down. It becomes a proxy for time, resource scarcity, and isolation.
- **Fatigue:** not stated directly — shown through slowed decisions, shorter sentences, reduced dialogue, or increased physical description.
- **Darkness:** progress from "dim" to "hard to see" to "navigating by memory and sound." Use the loss of light the way you would use a ticking clock.

## The Drone / Surveillance Pattern

When a civilian protagonist recognises adversary surveillance:

- Use a specific, concrete observation that separates trained recognition from paranoia (e.g. "drone altitude below roofline," "holding over pedestrian crossing," "moving through dark gaps").
- Let the protagonist name the pattern internally before they act on it. This validates their training for the reader without requiring exposition.
- The drone should return at intervals — not constantly present, but always *capable* of being present. Its return becomes a structural beat: "Above them, a faint hum returned."

## Dialogue as Pressure, Not Exposition

Thriller dialogue in this session followed these rules:

- Characters say what they need to say, not what the reader needs to know.
- Practical beats (directions, counts, check-ins) carry more weight than emotional disclosures.
- Emotional content is delivered through subtext or through what characters *choose not to say* (silence, a nod, a shift in posture).
- When a character does state a fear or intention (Stuart telling Kirsty about hostages), it functions as a decision point — the story changes because the information moved.

## The List as Artifact

Kirsty's written list (hotel guest census) serves multiple functions at once:
1. Establishes her practical competence and accounting background.
2. Seeds evidence that will matter later (audit discrepancy in Act 2).
3. Gives her agency without requiring tactical action.
4. Creates a physical object the reader remembers.

When seeding a later payoff, make the artifact visible early but frame it as in-character behaviour first (she's an accountant; of course she keeps lists). The later reveal recontextualises it.

## Crowd Herding Without Complicity

When writing a scene where civilians are being herded toward a trap:

- The protagonist must recognise the pattern early but lack the evidence to act.
- The decision to follow the crowd is a deliberate choice — not naivety, but information-gathering. "Staying in the crowd is the only way to see what happens before it happens to us."
- The protagonist's hand on the partner's arm (not grabbing, not holding) signals shared awareness without removing agency.
- The shapers in the crowd should be visible enough to be noticed, not visible enough to be caught. The protagonist should file them, not confront them.
